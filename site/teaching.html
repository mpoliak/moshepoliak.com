Teaching & mentoring

My research asks how speakers package information so that a listener can recover it; teaching is the same problem in practice. As a teacher, my goal is to give my students a compass and teach them how to read it. Through regular reflection, I help students develop autonomy in their work and independent, critical decision-making, and I give them space to find what makes their neurons pop.

Mentoring research

As an undergraduate at Harvard, I was advised by Mahzarin Banaji, and I am so indebted to her. Since 2023, I have mentored more than 30 undergraduate researchers from MIT, Wellesley, and Harvard, through MIT’s UROP program, the MSRP-Bio summer program, and funded positions. Many cross-linguistic studies started this way: students whose native language is not English adapt an English experiment to their own language, which is how our production experiments came to span 17 languages.

  1. Goals, set together

    Each semester begins with a written reflection. The student sets out their learning goals, I set out the project’s goals, and we build a roadmap together, revisiting it at midsemester and at the end.

  2. From guidance to independence

    In a first semester, I guide students closely. Students who stay take on more of the decisions, much like junior graduate students, with growing ownership of the design and materials.

  3. Setting the bar high

    When I started mentoring, I worried about overwhelming students and set the bar too low. I learned that students benefit from a challenge they are not sure they can meet: it shows that I trust their abilities, and invites them to surpass expectations.

Getting involved

Interested in research on language?

Undergraduates from any major are welcome, and no prior research experience is needed. If you speak a language other than English, there may be a study to run in it, but that is not a requirement.

To start a conversation, email moshepol@mit.edu with a few lines about what interests you.
