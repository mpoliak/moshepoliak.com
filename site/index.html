There are many ways to say the same thing.

Human civilization is built on language: the process of encoding our internal representations into an auditory, visual, or tactile code that a comprehender can perceive and use to infer the contents of our minds. Yet, it is surprising that linguistic communication succeeds at all given that the code itself—words and their combinations—vastly underdetermines meaning: the same words can communicate distinct things based on the manner and context in which they are said. My research tries to understand how communication is successful despite such an underdetermined code, investigating how linguistic communication can be explained by domain-general cognitive and communicative principles.

I pursue this program by applying rational inference to language, building on the noisy-channel framework (Levy, 2008; Gibson et al., 2013). Specifically, I posit that the goal of linguistic communication is for the comprehender to successfully infer the meaning that was intended by the producer, which they achieve via Bayesian reasoning over a noisy communication channel. By building and testing models rooted in this framework, I can explain how multiple linguistic phenomena fall out of comprehenders performing Bayesian inference, and producers anticipating these inferences. Through this approach, I seek to answer central, defining questions in language research: How do comprehenders manage to infer what the producer intended given an underdetermined code and a noisy signal? How do producers shape how they phrase an utterance to maximize the comprehender’s chance of success? How do general cognitive and communication principles bear on long-standing puzzles in linguistics?

I approach these questions with a diverse toolkit, relying on behavioral, computational, and neural methods. I design and build cross-linguistic datasets because I believe that true cognitive principles are shared by all humans and should not be language-dependent. Thus, any supposed cognitive communication-based principle should be observable across typologically diverse languages.

I am a PhD student in Brain and Cognitive Sciences at MIT, advised by Ted Gibson.

Current work

Comprehenders infer intended meaning through Bayesian reasoning.

Listeners combine the signal with expectations about both meaning and structure, from word order in Russian and Hindi to misheard song lyrics.

Producers shape their utterances to maximize communicative success.

Speakers make informative and corrected words longer, louder, and higher in pitch, in every language tested so far, and save informative words for later in the sentence, when the listener has more context to interpret them.

17 languages in experiments · 22 in conversation corpora · word order in English, Italian, and five more in progress
